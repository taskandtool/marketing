# Static ad formats

Static ad formats, one recipe each. Every recipe has the same lines: pick one and fill it from the idea and the claims ledger (`claims.md`). The examples use a made-up business, Harlow Joinery, a Bristol workshop that makes fitted kitchens. Reuse the copy patterns in brackets; an example only shows one pattern filled in. Every number in an example is made up: replace it with a figure from the ledger or drop it. Copy-space is the part of the picture kept clear for the words.

## Where this list comes from

The 45 formats below are the static formats named by at least two of these catalogues, checked on 7 Sep 2026: Motion's visual-format library (113 named formats, tagged across $1.29 billion of spend and 578,750 creatives in its Creative Benchmarks 2026), Motion's static-ads playbook, Common Thread Collective's 30 types, Demand Curve's format lesson, Contrast Digital's 13 styles, Superscale's static-ad guide, Hawky, Backlinko's 14 examples, Adrio's 7 formats and 12 examples, Konstant Kreative's prompt types, Fraggell's six, and Alex Cooper's (Adcrate) public writing on his 40-format static playbook (the playbook itself is behind a sign-in). Inside each group, the formats named by the most catalogues come first. Hit rates and spend shares are Motion's own figures from that benchmark and are not independently verified. No other performance figure below is verified unless it says so.

Every description, recipe and example below is written for this repository. Nothing is copied from those sources: what was taken from them is the name of a format (industry vocabulary that no one owns) and, where a figure is quoted, the figure with its source named beside it. Motion's benchmark across all formats: text-only and product-image-with-text creatives lead both hit-rate leaderboards. The offer-first banner is 21.9% of creatives and 29.3% of spend, at an 8.6% hit rate. Testimonials are 13.3% of spend at 6.5%. Some formats do better than their share of use: the letter (about 1.7x), unconventional text placement (about 1.5x), the post-it (about 1.3x) and celebrity or expert endorsement (2.1x). Lo-fi is 14% of ads and 42% of top performers.

## How to choose

- **Make distinct concepts, not variants.** Since Meta rolled out its Andromeda ranking worldwide (Oct 2025), an ad set does better with 8 to 15 ads on different angles than with one ad in twelve colourways. Keep similarity between concepts in an ad set under about 40% (Segwise figure, unverified). Vary the shape of the copy too: a question, a list, a quote, a statement.
- **Funnel stage** in each recipe's When line: top means cold (has not heard of us), mid means warm (has seen us or has the problem in mind), bottom means hot (has visited, enquired or abandoned).

## The recipes

### Us vs them
When: Mid funnel. The buyer is comparing options, and the business wins on criteria the buyer already cares about.
Structure: Two columns: "the alternative" on the left with crosses, the business on the right with ticks. Three to five rows of the buyer's real criteria, with a tick, a cross or a short value in each cell.
Copy: The header names the two columns. The rows are the buyer's criteria, not ours. Example: column heads "A flat-pack kitchen" and "Harlow Joinery". Rows: "Made to fit your walls: yes / cut down on site", "Carcass: 18 mm birch ply / chipboard", "Who fits it: the joiner who made it / whoever the retailer sends", "Lead time: 8 weeks / 2 to 12 weeks", "Repairable in ten years: yes / discontinued range".
Visual: A flat table on a plain ground, with the brand colour on our column header only. Optionally a small product photo above the table. The table needs no other copy-space.
Why it works: The buyer sees the comparison they were going to make anyway, set out on our terms.
Pitfalls: Naming a competitor risks a platform disapproval and a trademark complaint, and the ad sounds like an attack. Compare against a category ("a showroom chain", "flat-pack"). Every cell must be true for the whole category, not one bad example. Leave out rows the buyer does not care about (our founding year). More than five rows is unreadable at feed size.

### Before/after
When: Mid to bottom funnel. The business can show a visible change with its own photos.
Structure: Two frames side by side or stacked, labelled Before and After, with one short line under or over them.
Copy: "[What it was] to [what it is], [time or place]". Example: "A 1970s galley in Bishopston, before and after. Eight weeks from survey to fit." For a service with nothing to photograph: "Before: three quotes, three drawings, three different measurements. After: one joiner measures, draws and fits."
Visual: The two photographs at the same angle and crop, and in the same light where possible. Copy-space is a band across the top or bottom, or the labels only.
Why it works: Readers see the difference before they decide whether to read.
Pitfalls: Meta restricts before/after for health, weight and skincare (allowed only for general cosmetic use, 18+, no close-ups implying a flaw). Pinterest bans the format outright. Never generate or composite the "after" of a real job; if the pair is illustrative, say so on the image. Mismatched angles or lighting look fake.

### Testimonial or review screenshot
When: Warm audiences. The business has at least one specific, sourced review.
Structure: The review as a screenshot or a quote card, the reviewer's first name and town, a small product or job photo, and one proof detail under it.
Copy: The most specific sentence of the review as the hook, then the rest, then one fact. Example: "'They measured the room twice and the drawers still close on a wall that isn't square.' Ruth, Redland. Google review, March 2026. Solid oak fronts, birch ply carcass, fitted in four days."
Visual: A real screenshot (Google, Checkatrade, Trustpilot) with the platform's chrome kept, or a quote card in the brand type. Copy-space is the card itself. Put the photo behind or beside it.
Why it works: A stranger describing a specific problem we solved is more believable than any claim we make ourselves.
Pitfalls: Vague praise ("great service, would recommend") has no hook; pick the review with a detail. Never invent a review or a name, and never edit the wording: FTC and ASA rules treat that as a fake endorsement. If the results are unusual, say what is typical. Variants: Social Proof Mashup (three to five short reviews stacked) and Social Comments (a post with its own comments).

### Sticky note
When: Top to mid funnel. One short message that should not look like an ad.
Structure: A single handwritten note on a plain or photographed ground, one to twelve words, the logo small or absent.
Copy: One thought, written the way a person writes a reminder. Example: "Measure the room before you order the kitchen. Or we will." Or: "Drawers that still close in 2036."
Visual: A yellow or brand-tinted note in handwritten type, or a real note photographed on a worktop, a fridge or a site wall. Copy-space is the note.
Why it works: In a feed of polished content, it looks like a person's own thought.
Pitfalls: Use one note and no headline. A polished "handwritten" font on a perfect gradient looks like an ad again. The message must make sense alone, with no explanation on the image.

### Native-UI mimic
When: Mid funnel. A familiar moment of the buyer's problem can be shown as something that happens on their phone.
Structure: A faithful copy of one phone interface with the message inside it: a text thread, a notification, a Notes app page, an AirDrop prompt, a checkout screen, an email, a calendar entry.
Copy: One familiar pain in the interface's own voice, then one specific benefit. Examples for Harlow Joinery:
- Text thread: "Fitter's cancelled again" / "Third time?" / "Third time." / "Harlow send the joiner who built it. Same person, start to finish."
- Notification: "Harlow Joinery: Your kitchen is cut. Fitting starts Monday 14th."
- Notes app: title "Kitchen quotes", lines "Showroom chain: 3 visits, still no drawing", "Harlow: measured Tuesday, drawing Friday, price on the drawing".
- AirDrop: "Harlow Joinery would like to share 'Your kitchen, drawn to your walls.pdf'".
- Checkout: line items "Survey: £0", "Drawings: included", "Fitting by the maker: included".
- Email: subject "Your kitchen is in the workshop", preview "Photos of the oak fronts attached, fitting in 12 days."
- Calendar: "Survey, Harlow Joinery, Thursday 10:00, 45 min. They measure, you keep the drawings."
Visual: The interface at real proportions on a plain ground or in a phone photo. Copy-space is the interface. Put nothing outside it except a small logo when the interface does not show the name.
Why it works: People read these screens first, out of habit.
Pitfalls: Copy the interface exactly. With a wrong font, wrong spacing or a made-up interface, readers see an ad. They also spot generic dialogue ("Hey, have you tried Harlow?") as an ad on the first line. Do not fake a real person's name or a real notification from another brand. Keep to one screen.

### Tweet or Reddit screenshot
When: Top to mid funnel. The business has a post by a customer or the owner, or can write one in the owner's voice and publish it first.
Structure: A screenshot of a single post, or a post with one reply, on a plain ground.
Copy: Write it the way people talk about the problem in public, not the way we describe the product. Example (owner's post): "Fitted a kitchen today where the previous fitters had scribed the plinth to a floor that isn't there any more. Measure the room you have, not the plan." In the primary text, add 150 to 250 words of problem, agitation and solution for the 35+ Facebook feed audience.
Visual: The post at real proportions with its real chrome. Copy-space is the post.
Why it works: Buyers already talk about the category this way. They read the post as a conversation.
Pitfalls: Publish the post for real before taking the screenshot; a screenshot of a post that does not exist is fabricated proof. Never use a stranger's post without permission. Do not add a headline outside the screenshot.

### Founder note or letter
When: Top to mid funnel. The owner started the business for a reason a buyer would recognise.
Structure: Text only, set as a letter or a note and signed with a first name. Optionally a small photo of the owner or the workshop.
Copy: Why the owner started, then the old pain, then one proof, then the ask. Example: "I started Harlow Joinery after fitting other people's kitchens for eleven years and watching the same thing go wrong: the person who measured was never the person who fitted. So here the same joiner does both. Forty kitchens so far, none of them level floors. If you want yours measured properly, book a survey. Tom."
Visual: A plain ground and brand type at reading size, with the signature as the only decoration. Copy-space is the whole image.
Why it works: Few people dismiss an owner's own reason the way they dismiss a claim. They also have someone to reply to.
Pitfalls: Avoid corporate tone ("our mission", "we pride ourselves"). More than about 80 words on the image is unreadable in the feed; move the rest to the primary text. The proof must be in the ledger.

### Feature callout with arrows
When: Mid funnel. The product has three to five physical details that match benefits the buyer cares about.
Structure: One product photo with three to five short labels, each joined by a line or arrow to the detail it names.
Copy: Each label is a benefit with the feature as the reason, six words or fewer. Example labels on a kitchen photo: "Solid oak fronts, oiled not lacquered", "18 mm birch ply, not chipboard", "Soft-close runners rated 100,000 cycles", "Scribed to your wall, no filler strip". Optionally an offer band at the bottom (Motion found an offer-first banner on about half of these).
Visual: A clean product or job photo with room around the subject for labels. Copy-space is that margin.
Why it works: The reader gets the list of benefits without reading a list.
Pitfalls: Five labels at most. A feature without its benefit ("18 mm ply") means nothing to a buyer. Each label must point at the real detail.

### Press "as seen in"
When: Mid to bottom funnel, for higher-value purchases where trust is the obstacle.
Structure: One product or job photo, with a row of outlet logos or a single quoted headline and the outlet's name.
Copy: The outlet's own words, attributed. Example: "'A Bristol workshop that measures twice.' Avonmere Echo, May 2026." Or a logo row (Avonmere Echo, Lintel & Beam, The Renovator's Quarterly), only if each has covered the business.
Visual: The photo above, the press band below. Copy-space is the band.
Why it works: A third party has already vouched for the business.
Pitfalls: Every logo needs a real article, linked in the ledger. Outlets the buyer does not know add nothing. A paid placement is not press; label it if you use one.

### Offer stack / price anchor
When: Warm and retargeting audiences. There is a concrete offer with a deadline or a limit.
Structure: The offer as the header, three to five stacked items with a value each, the price or the saving, the deadline, one line of risk reversal and the call to action.
Copy: Example: "Autumn fitting slots. Survey and drawings (usually £250): included. Fitting by the joiner who made it: included. Ten-year guarantee on carcasses: included. Four slots left before Christmas. Book a survey."
Visual: A plain ground, or a photo faded behind a band. Copy-space is the full width, and the offer header is the largest type.
Why it works: Readers see what is included before the price, and the price feels earned.
Pitfalls: A fake deadline or a fake "usually £250" is a pricing claim the ASA will act on; the ledger must hold the normal price. Do not show this to a cold audience: they have no context for the offer. Keep the stack to five lines.

### Meme or reaction
When: Top funnel, only when the product fits the meme's logic without explanation.
Structure: A known meme template or a reaction photo, with one caption line placed the way the meme usually places it.
Copy: The joke is about the buyer's situation, and the product is the punchline or the caption. Example: two-panel reaction, top "Kitchen fitter says he'll be there Monday", bottom "Which Monday". Caption: "Harlow Joinery. The joiner who built it fits it. Dates on the drawing."
Visual: The meme image with its usual text placement. Copy-space is where the meme puts it.
Why it works: The reader recognises the format before the message and is already amused when they reach the name.
Pitfalls: If the meme needs the product explained, it fails. Meme images are often someone's copyright; use a photograph the business owns or a licensed stock image in the same shape. Memes date within weeks.

### Reasons-why listicle
When: Mid funnel. The business has three to five reasons, each a small proof and not an adjective.
Structure: A numbered list of three to five lines under a header that promises the count, with an optional photo.
Copy: "[N] reasons [audience] choose [business]", each reason a fact. Example: "3 reasons Bristol homeowners pick Harlow Joinery. 1. One joiner measures, draws and fits. 2. 18 mm birch ply carcasses, not chipboard. 3. You see the drawings before you pay a deposit." Text versions of this outperform talking-head versions.
Visual: A plain ground, or a photo with a solid band for the list. Copy-space is the band.
Why it works: From the count, the reader knows it is a short read. Each line is another reason to believe.
Pitfalls: Adjectives instead of proof ("1. Quality. 2. Service. 3. Value.") are the usual failure. More than five lines is a wall of text at feed size.

### Big statistic
When: Mid to late funnel, where trust is the obstacle. Not for cold audiences, who have no context for the number.
Structure: One number set very large, one line of context under it, the source small.
Copy: "[Number] [what it counts]. [Source or period]." Example: "0 callbacks in 2025. 38 kitchens fitted, none needed a return visit. Harlow Joinery job log."
Visual: A plain ground and brand type, with the number filling half the height. Copy-space is the whole image.
Why it works: A single number the reader can check is the fastest proof there is.
Pitfalls: The number must be in the ledger with its source and date. Readers doubt a percentage without a base ("98% satisfied"), and nobody can check it. Use one number per image.

### Problem-agitate-solve
When: Top funnel, the default cold format. The buyer has the problem but has not thought of us.
Structure: The problem named as the header, a picture of the problem, the specific result as the answer, the call to action.
Copy: The problem, then what it costs, then the result. Example: "Your kitchen fitter didn't measure the room. Now there's a 40 mm filler strip by the fridge and a drawer that hits the wall. We measure twice and cut in our own workshop. Book a survey." In video the split is about 60% problem and agitation, 40% solution. On a static, keep the problem to the header and the image.
Visual: A photo of the problem (the filler strip, the drawer that hits the wall) with the copy in a band. Copy-space is the band, top or bottom.
Why it works: The reader recognises their own situation before they recognise an ad.
Pitfalls: Stirring up a fear the buyer does not feel looks like a sales tactic. Do not imply anything about the reader personally (see the Meta personal attributes note under Who it's for).

### Product on a coloured ground / studio shot
When: Any stage. The workhorse for retargeting and catalogue ads.
Structure: The product or a detail of it on a flat colour or a studio backdrop, with the name and one line of copy.
Copy: The product's name and one fact. Example: "Oak shaker fronts, oiled. Made in Bristol, fitted by the maker." For retargeting, use the offer in place of the fact.
Visual: A generated or shot image of the object on a single brand-adjacent colour, with copy-space in a clear third of the frame. Do not use a tint of the product as the colour.
Why it works: The reader sees only the object and knows at a glance what is for sale.
Pitfalls: Never generate a product the business does not make or a room it did not fit; label illustrative renders as such. Cold audiences scroll past it without an offer or a fact.

### Who it's for (and not for)
When: Top funnel, where a demographic or role hook is the strongest opener. Lower volume, better cost per acquisition. Dara Denney's 2025 review of top hooks put demographic hooks first (secondary source, unverified).
Structure: A statement naming the audience by situation, optionally a second line naming who it is not for, and one photo.
Copy: "[Situation], this is for you. [One line of why.] Not for [situation]." Example: "Renovating a Victorian terrace in Bristol? This is for you. We make kitchens to fit walls that aren't square. Not for you if you want it next week: our lead time is eight weeks."
Visual: A photo of the situation (the terrace, the odd-shaped room) with the copy in a band. Copy-space is the band.
Why it works: People in that situation stop and the rest scroll on. The people who click are better leads.
Pitfalls: Meta's personal attributes policy: never assert or imply knowledge of the reader's race, religion, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, health, financial status, criminal record or name. "Over 60?" is banned; "Kitchens for retirement homes" is allowed. Write about the situation, the home or the product, never "your" condition. The "not for" line must be true and useful, not a humblebrag.

### Whiteboard or hand-drawn
When: Mid funnel. The topic is complex or dull, and a sketch explains it faster than a photo would.
Structure: A hand-drawn diagram or a photo of a whiteboard with marker writing, and one caption.
Copy: The drawing explains the idea and the caption names it. Example: a sketch of a wall with a 12 mm lean marked, a cabinet scribed to it, and the note "This is why we measure twice." Caption: "Old houses lean. We cut the cabinet to the wall, not the wall to the cabinet."
Visual: A real whiteboard or sheet of paper, photographed, or a drawn asset in a single marker colour. Copy-space is the board.
Why it works: People watch someone explain and forget it is an ad.
Pitfalls: A polished vector "sketch" loses the effect. More than one idea on the board is a lecture. The handwriting must be legible at 1080 wide.

### Four-panel checkerboard grid
When: Mid funnel. The business has two or more product photos and two or more short claims, and wants a carousel's density in one static.
Structure: A 2 x 2 grid alternating copy tiles and photo tiles, with one of each in every row.
Copy: Each copy tile is one claim of six words or fewer. Example: tile 1 "Made in our Bristol workshop", tile 2 a photo of the bench, tile 3 a photo of a fitted kitchen, tile 4 "Fitted by the joiner who made it".
Visual: Two photos and two solid copy tiles in the brand colour, all equal squares. Copy-space is the two solid tiles.
Why it works: In the time someone gives one image, they take in four proofs of one story.
Pitfalls: Four claims with no photo is a listicle done badly; keep the alternation. The photos must be the business's own. The whole grid must still make sense at thumbnail size.

### Anti-ad
When: Top funnel. The business can lead with a negative review, an admission or a joke at its own expense, and answer it.
Structure: The objection large and the answer smaller under it. Optionally the objection as a screenshot of a real comment.
Copy: The objection in the critic's words, then the reply. Example: "'Eight weeks is a long wait for a kitchen.' It is. It is also how long it takes to make one properly rather than pick one off a shelf. Book a survey and we will tell you the exact date." Variant: the "sceptic in the comments" rebuttal, a real doubting comment answered under the post.
Visual: A plain ground for the text version, or the real comment screenshot above a photo. Copy-space is the top half.
Why it works: Admitting the weakness first removes the reader's reason to distrust the rest.
Pitfalls: The objection must be one the business really faces; readers spot a straw man. Do not fabricate a negative review. The answer has to be a real answer, not a reframe.

### Advertorial
When: High-consideration purchases, mid funnel. The reader wants to learn before they buy.
Structure: An editorial headline, one photo, two or three bullet findings, a source line and a soft call to action.
Copy: A headline in the register of a magazine piece, not an offer. Example: "Why fitted kitchens in older Bristol homes fail at the walls. Three things a joiner checks before cutting: whether the floor is level (most are not), whether the walls are plumb (a 12 mm lean is common), where the services come through. Source: Harlow Joinery survey notes, 2025. Read the full checklist."
Visual: The photo cropped as a magazine would crop it, the headline over or under it, the bullets in a column. Copy-space is a third of the frame.
Why it works: Buyers read it because they have already searched for this answer.
Pitfalls: A wall of text at feed size: keep the bullets short and put the long version on the landing page. Label it as an ad where the platform requires. Each finding needs a source in the ledger, even if the source is our own job notes.

### First-frame iteration
When: Any stage, when a winning video exists. Make the video's opening frame into a static, then test new headlines on it.
Structure: A screenshot of the video's first frame with the spoken hook set as the on-screen headline, and nothing else.
Copy: The hook, word for word, as the headline. Example from a Harlow Joinery talking-head video: a frame of the joiner holding a scribed cabinet end, headline "This is the bit your fitter skips." To iterate, put each of the other tested hooks on the same frame.
Visual: The video frame, with copy-space where the video's caption was. Keep any lo-fi quality; it is part of why the video worked.
Why it works: The video has shown that people stop at this frame. From one frame you can test 30 headlines cheaply.
Pitfalls: Use only a video that performed. Do not tidy the frame; a graded, cropped version is a different ad. Check that the frame shows no face of a person who has not agreed to stills.

### Checklist
When: Mid funnel. The buyer is getting ready to choose, and a list of checks shows the business as the one that passes them.
Structure: A header naming the decision, four to six tick-box lines, and the business's name last or in the header.
Copy: "[N] things to check before [decision]", each line a check the business passes and many competitors do not. Example: "Before you order a fitted kitchen, check: the carcass material is named on the quote; the person measuring is the person fitting; the drawings arrive before the deposit; the guarantee covers the carcass as well as the hinges. Harlow Joinery passes all four."
Visual: A plain ground with the boxes ticked, or a printed checklist photographed on a worktop. Copy-space is the whole image.
Why it works: The reader takes the list to their next quote, and we wrote it in our favour.
Pitfalls: Leave out any line we would fail. Lines that only we could pass look rigged. Six lines at most.

### Myth vs fact
When: Mid funnel. A common belief in the category stops buyers, and the business can correct it with evidence.
Structure: Two blocks, "Myth" and "Fact", the myth struck through or in a muted colour, the fact in the brand colour, and a source line.
Copy: Example: "Myth: a made-to-fit kitchen costs twice a showroom one. Fact: our average 2025 kitchen came in 18% above the equivalent showroom range. That price includes the survey, drawings and fitting. Source: Harlow Joinery quotes, 2025." Or: "Myth: eight weeks is slow. Fact: showroom lead times in Bristol ran 2 to 12 weeks in 2025, and ours includes fitting."
Visual: A plain ground with two stacked blocks, or a photo behind a band. Copy-space is the full image.
Why it works: The reader already believes the myth. Correcting it changes their mind without asking them to take a new claim on trust.
Pitfalls: The fact needs a source in the ledger; nobody believes a myth corrected with an opinion. Do not name a competitor as the source of the myth. Health and finance myths carry policy risk; keep to the product.

### Quote card
When: Any stage. A single sentence from the owner, a customer or a third party carries the whole message.
Structure: The sentence large, the attribution small, a plain or textured ground, no photo needed.
Copy: The quote and who said it, with the source for a customer or the press. Example (owner): "'We cut the cabinet to the wall, not the wall to the cabinet.' Tom Harlow, Harlow Joinery." Example (customer): "'The kitchen matched the drawing to the millimetre.' Priya, Southville, Google review, June 2026."
Visual: Brand type at display size, with quotation marks as the only ornament. Copy-space is the whole image.
Why it works: One good sentence takes no longer to read than a glance at a photo.
Pitfalls: Customer quotes must be real and unedited. An owner "quote" that is a slogan is a statement; set it as one and drop the marks. Over 20 words needs smaller type and no longer works as a quote card.

### FAQ / "people ask us"
When: Mid funnel. The same question comes up in every enquiry, and the answer is a reason to buy.
Structure: The question as the header in the customer's words, the answer under it, and one photo or a plain ground.
Copy: "People ask us: [question]. [Answer in two sentences.]" Example: "People ask us: do you fit kitchens in flats with no lift? Yes. We make the carcasses in sections that fit a stairwell and assemble them in the room. Thirty-one flats in Clifton and Redland so far."
Visual: A plain ground with the question in the brand colour, or a photo of the situation the question is about. Copy-space is the top half.
Why it works: People stop for a question they have already asked themselves.
Pitfalls: A question nobody asks ("What makes Harlow different?") is a slogan in disguise; take questions from the enquiry inbox or `public/faq.md`. The answer must be true in every case, or say when it is not.

### Offer-first banner
When: Bottom and mid funnel, and any retargeting. The most used and most funded static format (Motion: 21.9% of creatives, 29.3% of spend, 8.6% hit rate). Use it when there is a real offer with a real end date.
Structure: The offer as the biggest thing on the frame (a number, a word), one line of what it applies to, the terms small, and the call to action.
Copy: "[The offer, as a number or two words]. [What it is on]. [Until when or for whom]." Example: "£300 off. Any kitchen drawn in October. Ends 31 October."
Visual: A plain ground in the brand's colour, or a product photo behind a scrim. No scene is needed.
Why it works: A warm reader already knows the business, and the offer is the only news for them. Put nothing else on the frame.
Pitfalls: Fake deadlines and sales that never end; an offer with no way to claim it; a pile of urgency words; showing it to cold traffic with no context.

### Headline-only (text-only)
When: Any stage. Motion's top asset type by hit rate. Use it when the claim is stronger than any picture the business has.
Structure: One line of large type on a plain ground, the business name small, and a call to action or none.
Copy: "[The claim in the customer's words, twelve words or fewer]." Example: "The people who draw your kitchen are the people who fit it."
Visual: None. The ground and the type are the ad. Use the accent colour once at most.
Why it works: In a feed of pictures, readers stop at a plain sentence and read it in a second.
Pitfalls: A vague claim ("quality you can trust"); a headline that needs a second line; decorative type; a gradient ground.

### Lifestyle / product in use
When: Top and mid funnel. The product or the work in use, in the customer's own setting.
Structure: A photograph with the words in a band at the bottom or top. The product or the work is the subject; show a person only by their hands or back.
Copy: "[The outcome for the person, one line]. [The call to action]." Example: "Cooking in it by Friday. Book a workshop visit."
Visual: The owner's own photo first. Otherwise generate a photograph with the setting from the Imagery block in `brand/visual-identity.md`. Copy-space is the plainest third of the frame.
Why it works: The reader sees the product in a life that looks like theirs, not on a white ground.
Pitfalls: Stock lifestyle (a stranger smiling); a scene so styled it looks like a catalogue; a product too small to recognise.

### Phone snapshot (UGC-style photo, the ugly ad)
When: Top funnel. A photo that looks like a customer or the owner took it on a phone, with one caption chip and nothing else.
Structure: The photo fills the frame. One short caption in a white chip, like a phone's own caption; no headline and no logo.
Copy: "[One sentence a person would type under their own photo]." Example: "Day three and the doors are on."
Visual: Use a real phone photo (the owner's, or a customer's with permission). If you generate one, prompt for an unposed phone snapshot (flash, tilt, a bit of mess) and note in `creative.md` that it is illustrative.
Why it works: It does not look like an ad, and readers do not skip it as one. Lo-fi is 42% of top performers.
Pitfalls: Making it look designed; a caption that sells; a generated photo passed off as a customer's.

### How it works (mechanism, infographic)
When: Mid funnel, for a product or service whose method is the difference. Also the "three steps" ad.
Structure: A title, then three or four numbered steps down the frame, each a short title and one line.
Copy: "[Title: the promise]. 1 [step] 2 [step] 3 [step]." Example: "How a kitchen gets fitted in three days. 1 We measure. 2 We build it in the workshop. 3 The same two people fit it."
Visual: None needed; the numbers and the type are enough. Add a small photo per step only when the owner has them.
Why it works: A method is easy to believe, and three steps are easy to remember.
Pitfalls: More than four steps; features dressed up as steps; a diagram the reader must study.

### Social proof mashup (trust stack)
When: Mid and bottom funnel. Several kinds of proof on one frame: a rating, a count, two short quotes, a badge.
Structure: The rating big with the count beside it and the source small, then two quotes in frames, then the call to action.
Copy: "[Rating] [count of reviews] on [source]. "[Quote one]" [who]. "[Quote two]" [who]." Example: "4.9 from 118 reviews on Google. "They measured twice and it shows." Ruth, Redland."
Visual: None; every element is text from the ledger. Never use a star graphic the image model painted.
Why it works: One review can be luck. A rating, a count and two voices look like a pattern.
Pitfalls: Any number not in the ledger; rounding a rating up; edited quotes; a source nobody can check.

### Comment response (reply to a comment)
When: Mid funnel. A real question or objection from a comment, quoted, with the business's reply under it.
Structure: The comment as a card (the commenter's first name and their words), the reply below it marked as the business's, and a small business label.
Copy: "[The comment, verbatim]. [Business] replied: [the answer, plain, one claim]." Example: "Do you do just the doors? Harlow Joinery replied: Yes, and we fit them the same week we make them."
Visual: A comment-thread layout; no picture.
Why it works: Someone else asks the reader's question in public, and the business answers it.
Pitfalls: Inventing the comment; editing the commenter's words; a reply that turns into a pitch; using a real name without permission.

### Grid (starter pack, the range, an education grid)
When: Top and mid funnel. Four to nine small pictures with captions that together say "this is what we do" or "these are the options".
Structure: A 2x2 or 3x3 grid of frames, each a photo with a short caption chip, a small headline above, and an optional call to action.
Copy: "[Headline: the set]. [Caption per cell, two or three words]." Example: "This year's kitchens. Southville. Clifton. Bedminster. Bishopston."
Visual: The owner's photos, one per cell. Without photos, the cells hold words only and the format becomes a listicle.
Why it works: Readers see the range at a glance, and they look at a grid longer than at one picture.
Pitfalls: Cells that all show the same photo; more than nine; captions that are sentences; a grid used to hide that there is no single strong picture.

### Expert or endorsement (celebrity, authority)
When: Mid funnel, when a named person with standing has said something true about the business and given permission. Motion's highest spend-to-use ratio (2.1x), rarely available to a small business.
Structure: The quote large, the person's name and standing beneath it, and their photo only with permission.
Copy: ""[The quote, verbatim]." [Name], [what makes them worth listening to]." Example: ""The only fitters I recommend." Jane Doe, building surveyor, Bristol."
Visual: The quote set large, with a photo of the person only with written permission.
Why it works: The reader trusts the endorser's judgement more than the advertiser's claim.
Pitfalls: A paid endorsement that does not say so (FTC and ASA rules); a quote taken from a review and presented as an endorsement; a face without permission.

### Benefit stack (value proposition, specs)
When: Mid and bottom funnel. Three to six ticked benefits, or the specs, under a headline.
Structure: A headline, then ticked lines, then the call to action.
Copy: "[Headline: the offer]. ✓ [benefit] ✓ [benefit] ✓ [benefit]." Example: "A kitchen from the workshop. ✓ Measured by the maker. ✓ Built in birch ply. ✓ Fitted in three days."
Visual: None, or a product photo beside the list on a 1.91:1.
Why it works: A warm reader wants the list; it is the comparison they were going to make.
Pitfalls: Features with no benefit; more than six; adjectives in the list; a benefit that is not in the ledger.

### Urgency (countdown, seasonal, event)
When: Bottom funnel, with a real date: a season's last slots, an event, a price rise.
Structure: The date or the count as the biggest thing, what happens on that date, and the call to action.
Copy: "[What ends and when]. [What to do before then]." Example: "Six fitting slots left before Christmas. Book a visit this week."
Visual: The banner ground; no scene.
Why it works: With a true deadline, the reader stops putting off the decision.
Pitfalls: A deadline that moves; a countdown to nothing; a pile of urgency words ("hurry, last chance, now"); running it more than once a quarter.

### App or interface mockup (in-app proof shot)
When: Mid funnel, for anything with a screen or a result shown on one: a quote, a booking, a dashboard, a delivery notice.
Structure: A phone-shaped card with a title and a few rows (label and value), one highlighted line, and the business name small.
Copy: "[Screen title]. [Row: label, value] x3. [The line that matters, in the accent]." Example: "Your drawing. Measured: Tuesday. Drawn: Friday. Price: on the drawing. Fitting: 3 days."
Visual: A real screenshot of the real product. Never an interface painted by an image model.
Why it works: A screen looks like evidence to most people.
Pitfalls: A mocked-up screen for a product that has no screen; numbers not in the ledger; a fake brand's interface.

### Editorial (magazine style)
When: Top funnel, for a considered purchase. A still image with a magazine's restraint.
Structure: A photograph with a lot of empty space, a small headline in the margin or a thin band, and the business name as a small label.
Copy: "[A short line, under eight words, that reads like a caption]." Example: "Made on Cumberland Road."
Visual: The owner's best photograph, or a generated scene with a wide plain margin for the words. Keep the text band thin.
Why it works: In a feed of ads, people take it for content.
Pitfalls: So much restraint that the picture has no message; a stock image; a logo bigger than the words.

### Flat lay
When: Mid funnel, for a product with parts, a kit, a bundle or a menu of options.
Structure: The items arranged on a plain ground and shot from directly above, evenly spaced, with one label line.
Copy: "[What is in it, as a count or a name]." Example: "Everything in the fitting kit."
Visual: The owner's product photo from above (or a generated one with the camera directly above), with the brand's colour as the ground.
Why it works: The reader sees everything at once, in order.
Pitfalls: Items too small to make out; a busy ground; props that are not for sale.

### Text in context (a sign, a billboard, a mug)
When: Top funnel. The headline is on a real object in the scene: a sign, a van, a wall, a board. Motion: unconventional text placement does about 1.5x better than its share of use.
Structure: A photograph in which the words are part of the scene, on a flat surface (a painted board, a van side, a plain wall).
Copy: "[The claim, six words or fewer]." Example: "Kitchens fitted by their makers."
Visual: A generated photograph. Let the image model paint the words only when it renders text cleanly, and check every letter.
Why it works: People read a sign in a scene sooner than a headline on a card.
Pitfalls: A surface that is not flat; text that competes with the object.

### Objection-led (skeptic to customer)
When: Mid funnel. The reader's doubt comes first, then the answer.
Structure: The objection as the headline, the answer as the body, and the call to action. Or two columns: the doubt and the fact.
Copy: ""[The objection, as the reader would say it]." [The answer, one claim from the ledger]." Example: ""A workshop kitchen costs more." About 18% more than a showroom's, and that includes the survey, drawings and fitting."
Visual: None; the words set large on the brand's ground.
Why it works: Once you name the reader's doubt, they listen to the answer.
Pitfalls: Straw objections; an answer that is a slogan; an objection about a competitor by name.

### Illustration (cartoon, comic strip)
When: Top funnel, when the brand is drawn rather than photographed, or the idea is abstract.
Structure: A drawn scene or a two-panel strip with the words in a plain band. If the model draws the words, check them letter by letter.
Copy: "[The idea in one line]." Example: "Two people, one kitchen, start to finish."
Visual: An illustration. Use this format only when the Imagery block says the brand is drawn.
Why it works: Readers see a drawing as a point of view and never mistake it for stock.
Pitfalls: A cartoon style the brand does not own; model-drawn text nobody checked; cuteness.

### Case study (results)
When: Bottom funnel, business-to-business or a considered purchase. Two or three numbers with what they measure and the source.
Structure: A headline naming the job, then two or three big numbers each with a label, the source small, and the call to action.
Copy: "[Headline: the job]. [Number] [what it measures] x3. [Source]." Example: "The Clifton kitchen. 3 days to fit. 0 return visits. 1 drawing."
Visual: None; the numbers are the picture.
Why it works: Specific numbers about one job are easier to believe than a claim about all jobs.
Pitfalls: Numbers not in the ledger; an unusual result shown without saying what is typical; a client named without permission.

### Giveaway or contest
When: Top funnel, for reach and list building, within the platform's rules.
Structure: The prize as the biggest thing, how to enter in one line, the closing date and the terms line.
Copy: "Win [the prize]. [How to enter]. Closes [date]." Example: "Win a drawing of your kitchen. Book a visit before 30 October. Closes 31 October."
Visual: The banner ground or a photo of the prize.
Why it works: A prize is an offer with no price.
Pitfalls: "Tag a friend" or "share to enter" (Meta demotes these as engagement bait); no terms; a prize unrelated to the business.

### Badge or certification
When: Mid funnel, when the business holds a real accreditation, award or guarantee.
Structure: The badge's claim as a headline, the issuer as a label, and one line on what it means for the reader.
Copy: "[The accreditation]. [Issuer]. [What it means for you]." Example: "Guild of Master Craftsmen. Since 2019. Every kitchen inspected before you pay."
Visual: The real badge file from the owner as the mark. Never a badge drawn by a model.
Why it works: The reader sees at a glance that a third party approves.
Pitfalls: A badge the business does not hold; a drawn badge; an award with no issuer.

## Copy rules for any static

1. **Hook first.** The first line on the image is the reason to stop, never the business name. Put the name small or leave it to the profile.
2. **Policy on every draft.** Personal attributes (Meta): describe the product or the situation, never the reader. Before/after: not for health, weight or skincare. Competitors: a category, never a name. Endorsements: real, unedited, and disclosed if paid.
3. **Landing page matches the hook.** The page the ad links to must say the same thing in the same words, or the click is wasted.
4. **Reviews and letters** can run to 80 words at reading size.
